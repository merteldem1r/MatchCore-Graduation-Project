How an exchange and an order book actually work

Before any code or tech stack, you need a clear picture of the problem. This page builds it from zero: what an order is, how an order book is arranged, the rules that decide who trades with whom, and where a matching engine fits in a real exchange.

1.1What is an exchange?

An exchange is a marketplace where buyers and sellers of the same thing meet: shares of a company (e.g. THYAO on Borsa İstanbul, AAPL on NASDAQ), currencies, futures, crypto. Each tradeable thing is called an instrument or symbol.

People don't negotiate with each other directly. Everybody sends their wishes to the exchange ("I'll buy at this price", "I'll sell at that price"). The exchange keeps a list of all the wishes that haven't been satisfied yet, and when a buyer and a seller agree on price, it makes them trade.

The program at the center of the exchange that does this pairing is the matching engine. Everything else (websites, broker apps, charts) is built around it.

Who sends orders?

Normal investors don't connect directly. They go through a broker (bank or brokerage app), which forwards their orders to the exchange. Big firms (funds, market makers, HFT firms) connect with low-latency direct connections.

Market makers

Firms that always keep both a buy and a sell order in the book, so anyone who wants to trade has someone to trade with. They earn the small gap between the two prices. That's why they care about speed so much.
Key idea: one order book per symbol. A real engine handles thousands of symbols, but each symbol's book is independent. Our v1 can start with a single symbol.

1.2What is an order?

order_id 1042
symbol THYAO
side BUY
type LIMIT
price 50.00
quantity 100
timestamp 09:00:01.000123

An order is a small message. It means: "Buy 100 THYAO, and pay at most ₺50.00 per share."

  • side: buy or sell.
  • price: for a buy it's the maximum you'll pay; for a sell it's the minimum you'll accept.
  • quantity: how many shares.
  • timestamp / sequence: when the exchange received it. This decides who goes first when two orders have the same price.

1.3The order book

PriceQtyOrder (arrival)
50.3080#6 (4th)
50.20300#4 (1st)
50.1050#5 (2nd) ← best ask
— spread = 0.10 —
50.00100#1 (1st) ← best bid
50.0040#3 (3rd)
49.90200#2 (2nd)

The book has two sides:

  • Bids (buyers), sorted from highest price down. The top one is the best bid.
  • Asks (sellers), sorted from lowest price up. The top one is the best ask.

The gap between best bid and best ask is the spread. In a normal resting book, best bid < best ask. If they overlapped, those orders would already have traded.

All orders at the same price form a price level. Inside a level, orders wait in a FIFO queue (first in, first out), like a line at a bank. Level 50.00 above holds #1 then #3.

Key idea: this is where data structures come in. You need (1) the sides sorted by price, (2) a FIFO queue inside each price level, and (3) a fast way to find order #1042 when someone cancels it. These are the three parts your advisor wants you to compare.
Tick size: prices can't be anything. They move in fixed steps called ticks (e.g. ₺0.01). So engines usually store price as an integer number of ticks (50.00 → 5000) instead of a float. That avoids rounding bugs, and it's what makes the "array of price levels" idea possible later.

1.4The matching rules

1 · When do two orders trade?

When a buyer's price ≥ a seller's price. We say the orders cross. A new buy at 50.10 crosses the ask at 50.10; a buy at 50.05 does not.

2 · Price priority

A new order always trades against the best price on the other side first. A buyer gets the cheapest seller; a seller gets the highest buyer.

3 · Time priority

At the same price, the order that arrived first trades first (FIFO). Rules 2 + 3 together = price-time priority.

4 · Trade price

The trade happens at the price of the order that was already resting in the book (the maker). The newcomer (the taker) may get a better price than it asked for.

5 · Partial fills

Quantities don't have to match. If you buy 120 and the best seller has 50, you trade 50, then continue with the next seller. Leftover on a limit order rests in the book.

6 · Determinism

Given the same orders in the same sequence, the engine must produce exactly the same trades every time. No randomness and no dependence on timing. This is what makes replay-based recovery possible.

1.5Worked examples

All start from the book in 1.3.

Example A · No trade

BUY LIMIT 30 @ 50.05
50.05 < best ask 50.10, so nothing crosses. The order rests as the new best bid at 50.05, and the spread shrinks to 0.05. No trade happens; this is still useful because the order adds liquidity.

Example B · Time priority inside a level

PriceQtyOrder
50.00100#1 → fully filled
50.0040 → 20#3 → partially filled
49.90200#2 untouched
SELL LIMIT 120 @ 49.90
  1. Best bid is 50.00 ≥ 49.90 → cross. At 50.00, #1 arrived first → TRADE 100 @ 50.00 with #1.
  2. 20 left. Next in line at 50.00 is #3 → TRADE 20 @ 50.00. #3 keeps 20 shares.
  3. Seller is fully filled. Note: the seller asked for ≥49.90 but got 50.00. That's price improvement, because the trade uses the resting order's price.

Example C · Market order walking the book

PriceQtyOrder
50.3080#6 untouched
50.20300 → 150#4 partial
50.1050#5 filled
BUY MARKET 200
  1. Best ask 50.10 → TRADE 50 @ 50.10 with #5.
  2. Next level 50.20 → TRADE 150 @ 50.20 with #4 (150 left on #4).
  3. Average price paid ≈ 50.175. A large market order moves the price, which is why traders worry about "market impact".

Example D · Cancel and modify

CANCEL #1: the engine has to find #1 quickly, remove it from the middle of the 50.00 queue, and #3 becomes first in line. Doing this in O(1) is exactly why your advisor mentioned an "intrusive list" and an order-id hash map.
MODIFY #3 → price 50.10: treated as cancel #3 + new order #3 at 50.10, so it goes to the back of the line at its new price. In this book it would actually cross the ask at 50.10 and trade.

1.7What the engine outputs: events

Look at the log in the simulator. For every input, the engine produces a list of events:

IN  NEW #7 SELL LIMIT 120 @ 49.90
OUT ACCEPTED #7
OUT TRADE 100 @ 50.00 buyer=#1 seller=#7
OUT TRADE 20 @ 50.00 buyer=#3 seller=#7
OUT FILLED #7

If you write down every input in order (the event log), you can rebuild the whole book later by feeding the same inputs into the engine again. That's deterministic replay, the fault-tolerance part your advisor wants. Saving the whole book every now and then (a snapshot) means you only have to replay from the last snapshot instead of from the beginning.

A real exchange is a whole system. Our project is the highlighted box, plus a simple way to feed it orders (a file or a generator), plus the event log for recovery, plus a separate dashboard that reads the outputs.

The sequencer is worth noticing: the engine is usually single-threaded per order book. Orders are processed one at a time, in one fixed order. That sounds slow, but it's simple, deterministic, and with good data structures it can process millions of orders per second. LMAX's well-known architecture is built on this idea; we'll read about it in Step 3.

1.9What this means for our version 1

This isn't decided yet; it's just to see how small the start can be:

  • One symbol, one order book, single-threaded.
  • Limit orders, market orders, cancel.
  • Price-time priority with partial fills.
  • Prices as integer ticks.
  • Input: a simple text file of orders. Output: printed events.
  • The simplest data structures first (e.g. std::map + std::list + std::unordered_map), just to be correct.

Every later feature (faster data structures, benchmarks, event log, snapshots, dashboard, maybe Raft) is an upgrade on top of this, and each upgrade can be measured against the previous version. That "v1 → v2 → v3, measured every time" story also makes a good thesis.

Glossary

Instrument / symbol
The thing being traded, e.g. THYAO.
Bid / Ask
A buy order / a sell order. "Ask" is also called "offer".
Best bid / best ask
Highest buy price / lowest sell price in the book. Together: the "top of book".
Spread
Best ask − best bid.
Price level
All resting orders at one price, kept in a FIFO queue.
Depth
How much quantity sits at each level, e.g. "top 5 levels". Dashboards show this.
Tick
Smallest allowed price step.
Resting order
An order waiting in the book.
Maker / taker
Maker = the resting order that provided liquidity. Taker = the incoming order that removed it.
Cross
When a buy price ≥ a sell price, so they can trade.
Fill / partial fill
An order (partly) executed.
Liquidity
How much is available to trade without moving the price a lot.
Price-time priority
Best price first; same price → earliest first. Also called FIFO matching.
Event log
The ordered record of all inputs, used to replay and recover.
Deterministic
Same inputs in same sequence → identical outputs, always.

Check yourself

Try to answer before opening. If any of these feel unclear, ask me about it before Step 2.

1. Best bid 50.00, best ask 50.10. A BUY LIMIT 100 @ 50.05 arrives. What happens?

No trade (50.05 < 50.10). It rests as the new best bid; spread becomes 0.05.

2. Two buy orders at 50.00: #1 (first) for 100, #3 (later) for 40. A SELL 60 @ 50.00 arrives. Who trades?

Only #1, for 60 shares (time priority). #1 keeps 40; #3 is untouched.

3. A BUY LIMIT 50 @ 51.00 arrives and the best ask is 50.10 with 50 shares. At what price is the trade?

50.10, the resting (maker) order's price. The buyer was willing to pay 51.00 but gets price improvement.

4. A market order can't be fully filled because the book runs out. What happens to the rest?

It's cancelled. Market orders never rest in the book, because they have no price to rest at.

5. Why do we store prices as integers instead of floats?

Floats can't represent many decimals exactly (0.1 + 0.2 ≠ 0.3), which would break price comparisons. Integer ticks are exact and also allow indexing price levels in an array.

6. Why must the engine be deterministic?

So that replaying the event log after a crash rebuilds exactly the same book, and so that tests and benchmarks are reproducible.
